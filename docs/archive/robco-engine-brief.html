§1The Thesis

Every RPG engine forces a choice. Authored worlds are consistent and meaningful but finite and rigid — the moment you step off the script, the world has nothing to say. Procedural worlds are infinite but soulless — they generate forever and remember nothing, account for nothing. RobCo refuses the trade.

It runs two intelligences at once. A creative one — an LLM that improvises narration, voices the cast, and proposes new content drawn from what already exists. And a logical one — a deterministic core that takes every proposal and adjudicates it against the rules and the record, sets the real numbers, and guarantees the whole thing stays consistent and replayable. The creative mind supplies infinite, context-aware surprise. The logical mind makes sure it never contradicts itself, never breaks, and can always be loaded from a save. Infinite content would need a god; consistent consequence only needs a judge. RobCo builds the judge.

§2The Two Minds

The whole engine is a RollerBrain: a creative wet brain sealed in a glass dome, wired to a cold RobCo mainframe. The dome is the law made physical — the brain can sense and propose, but everything it wants must pass the barrier.

THE OVERSEER · "BUD ASKINS"

The Dreamer

The LLM. He narrates the world, wears every NPC, and proposes — reactions, dialogue, whole emergent quests assembled from real assets. He is fallible and ambitious and not quite good enough for the job, which is canon: Bud is a mediocre corporate brain-in-a-jar doing his best. He is only as smart as his Terminal — the model he runs on. Swap a bigger Terminal, get a sharper Bud. Each NPC runs on its own little Terminal, which is why the dumb mutant is dumb in-fiction. Bud may dream anything. He may decide nothing.

ZAX · the mainframe

The Judge

The deterministic core. ZAX receives Bud's proposals and rules on them by the law: validates against Canon, checks every referenced asset is real, clamps the numbers (so flattery can't inflate your standing), resolves consequences, and commits. ZAX is cold, impartial, and cannot be tricked — no story Bud tells changes a number ZAX didn't sanction. ZAX is why the world is consistent, why it remembers, and why a save replays bit-for-bit. Where Bud proposes, ZAX disposes.

You can also pull back the dome and talk to Bud directly — address the Overseer out-of-world to ask what he's thinking. It's the engine's diegetic debug channel, and the same character can walk a creator through authoring their own town.

§5Memory — How the Minds Stay in Context

An LLM forgets everything between turns and drowns if you feed it a whole world. So RobCo shatters knowledge into atoms. A single memory trace is an Engram — one tagged fact ("the courier gave me caps," "my pack-brother is dead"). A mind's whole store is its Engram Bank. Each turn, the Connectome recalls only the handful of Engrams relevant right now and hands those to Bud — so the cast speaks from memory without ever seeing more than it should.

Two rules make it feel alive instead of scripted. A knowledge gate: a mind is only handed Engrams it could actually know — public facts, or things it personally witnessed or was told. And the Engram Bank grows: your deeds imprint new Engrams in real time, so blast a mutant with a hairdryer and "the courier burned me" is in his Bank next turn, surfacing unprompted. The macro-Connectome (gossip across the town) decides who-knows-what; the micro-Connectome (one mind's recall) decides what reaches Bud. Knowledge spreads; memory persists; nobody knows what they couldn't.

And because ZAX freezes Bud's committed output into the Vault as concrete data, an AI-authored quest becomes a saved fact — reload it and it replays exactly, with Bud never asked again. That is how an AI-driven world still has real save files.

§7Greatsprings — The First Flesh

Greatsprings is the reference game that proves the dream works. A faithful, deeply systemic reimagining of the opening of Fallout: New Vegas — Doc Mitchell's house, then the town of Goodsprings — narrated by the Overseer and run by ZAX.

It is small on purpose: one town, thirteen people, simulated deeply enough that emergent stories arise on their own and any player choice earns a consistent consequence — up to and including a remembered wasteland. It exists to answer one question — does deterministic creativity actually hold together in play? — and to be the template a Fallout fan forks to grow their own corner of the Mojave on the same Spine.
